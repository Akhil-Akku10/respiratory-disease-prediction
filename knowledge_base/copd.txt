DISEASE: Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

OVERVIEW
Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a chronic inflammatory lung disease that causes obstructed airflow from the lungs. It is characterized by long-term breathing problems and poor airflow. The main subtypes are emphysema and chronic bronchitis. COPD is progressive, meaning it typically worsens over time. It is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. COPD is primarily caused by long-term exposure to irritating gases or particulate matter, most often from cigarette smoke.

SYMPTOMS
- Persistent cough with mucus (chronic bronchitis)
- Shortness of breath, especially during physical activities
- Wheezing and chest tightness
- Frequent respiratory infections
- Blueness of the lips or fingernail beds (cyanosis)
- Fatigue and lack of energy
- Unintended weight loss (in later stages)
- Swelling in ankles, feet, or legs
- Barrel-shaped chest due to hyperinflation
- Increased mucus production

CAUSES AND RISK FACTORS
- Cigarette smoking (primary cause — responsible for 85-90% of COPD cases)
- Long-term exposure to secondhand smoke
- Occupational exposure to dust, chemicals, and fumes (coal dust, silica, grain dust)
- Air pollution (indoor biomass fuel burning, outdoor pollution)
- Genetic factors — Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD)
- History of childhood respiratory infections
- Age (most patients are 40 years or older)
- Asthma history

DIAGNOSIS
- Spirometry (FEV1/FVC ratio < 0.70 confirms airflow limitation)
- Chest X-ray (shows hyperinflation, flattened diaphragm, increased AP diameter)
- CT scan of the chest (more sensitive for detecting emphysema)
- Arterial blood gas analysis (ABG) to assess oxygen and CO2 levels
- 6-minute walk test to assess exercise capacity
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin level testing if genetic cause suspected
- Pulse oximetry for oxygen saturation monitoring

STAGING (GOLD Classification)
- GOLD 1 (Mild): FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted
- GOLD 2 (Moderate): 50% ≤ FEV1 < 80% predicted
- GOLD 3 (Severe): 30% ≤ FEV1 < 50% predicted
- GOLD 4 (Very Severe): FEV1 < 30% predicted

FATALITY AND PROGNOSIS
COPD is the third leading cause of death globally. It is incurable but manageable. The 5-year survival rate varies widely depending on severity. Moderate COPD has approximately 60-70% survival at 5 years. Severe COPD (GOLD 4) has a 5-year survival below 30%. Acute exacerbations significantly increase mortality risk. Patients who stop smoking show significantly slower disease progression.

TREATMENT AND MEDICATIONS
Bronchodilators (first-line therapy):
- Short-acting beta-agonists (SABA): Salbutamol (albuterol), Levalbuterol — for immediate relief
- Long-acting beta-agonists (LABA): Salmeterol, Formoterol, Indacaterol
- Short-acting muscarinic antagonists (SAMA): Ipratropium bromide
- Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA): Tiotropium (Spiriva), Umeclidinium, Aclidinium
- Combination LABA/LAMA: Umeclidinium/Vilanterol (Anoro Ellipta)

Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS):
- Fluticasone, Budesonide — used in combination with LABA for frequent exacerbators
- Triple therapy (ICS/LABA/LAMA): Fluticasone furoate/Umeclidinium/Vilanterol (Trelegy Ellipta)

Other Medications:
- Roflumilast (PDE4 inhibitor) — for severe COPD with chronic bronchitis
- Theophylline — for patients who cannot use inhaled therapy
- Azithromycin (long-term prophylactic antibiotic) — reduces exacerbation frequency
- N-acetylcysteine (mucolytic) — helps clear mucus
- Oral corticosteroids — short-term only for acute exacerbations

Oxygen Therapy:
- Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) for patients with resting PaO2 ≤ 55 mmHg
- Improves survival in severely hypoxemic patients

Surgical Options:
- Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) — for upper-lobe emphysema
- Bullectomy — removal of large bullae compressing normal tissue
- Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) with endobronchial valves
- Lung transplantation — for end-stage COPD

Pulmonary Rehabilitation:
- Exercise training, breathing techniques, nutritional counseling
- Significantly improves quality of life and reduces hospitalizations

HEALTH TIPS AND RECOVERY
1. Stop smoking immediately — the single most effective intervention to slow COPD progression; nicotine replacement therapy and varenicline (Chantix) can help
2. Practice pursed-lip breathing and diaphragmatic breathing exercises daily to reduce dyspnea
3. Get annual influenza vaccination and pneumococcal vaccine to prevent respiratory infections that worsen COPD
4. Avoid exposure to lung irritants: dust, smoke, chemical fumes, and air pollution
5. Maintain a healthy weight — obesity worsens breathlessness; underweight patients have worse prognosis
6. Stay physically active within your limits; pulmonary rehabilitation programs are highly beneficial
7. Monitor your symptoms and recognize early signs of exacerbation: increased breathlessness, change in sputum color, increased cough
8. Use your inhaler correctly — improper technique is the most common reason for treatment failure

EXACERBATIONS
Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) are sudden worsening of symptoms requiring additional treatment.
Triggers include respiratory infections (viral/bacterial), air pollution, and non-compliance with medications.
Treatment: increased bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone 40mg for 5 days), antibiotics if infection suspected, controlled oxygen therapy, non-invasive ventilation (NIV) if needed.
Frequent exacerbations (≥2/year) significantly accelerate lung function decline.
